\chapter{Methods}
\label{ch:methods}

This chapter describes the data, the four weighting models, and the analyses
built on them. The first half works with the peer ratings: the models, the
attack simulation used to test them, and the state cascade that sorts rating
matrices by whether they carry a readable signal. The second half works with
the students' reflective journals and the LLM pipeline used to read them.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

The data come from COMPSCI~399, the University of Auckland's Computer Science
capstone course. Students work in teams of five or six on a semester-long
software project. Across a semester each student completes several peer
feedback sessions, in which they rate their teammates, and several reflective
journals, in which they write about their own work and their team's.

This study uses five cohorts with both data sources (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).
A sixth cohort (2022~Semester~2) has journals but no peer-rating data and is not
used in any analysis reported here.

\subsection{Peer-rating data}
\label{sec:peer-data}

Each peer feedback session contains several questions, not all of them numeric.
Every session asks each student to estimate how much each team member has
contributed, on a scale where 100 is an equal share. The first and final
sessions also contain \emph{points-distribution} questions: each student
distributes a fixed pool of $10N$~points (50 for a five-person team, 60 for a
six-person team) across all team members, themselves included. The final
session has three such questions, one for each of the project's deliverables:
the source code, the group report, and the showcase poster.

This dissertation uses the three points-distribution questions from each
cohort's final session. They are the only questions that ask for the same
thing, in the same format, in every cohort, and each gives a complete
team-by-team rating matrix. \todo{Jos: confirm whether the final-session points
questions are the ones that set the IWF used for grading. If so, say so here ---
it is the strongest reason for the choice.}

Each question yields one $N \times N$ matrix per team, where entry $s_{ji}$ is
the score rater~$j$ gave to student~$i$. A student who did not submit leaves an
empty column. Teams in which half or more of the members did not submit are
dropped, which removes two teams from the 141 in the source files. The result is
$139 \times 3 = 417$ rating matrices. Self-scores are recorded in the matrix;
how each model treats them is stated in Section~\ref{sec:models}.

\subsection{Reflective journals}
\label{sec:journal-data}

Students submit reflective journals through the course's learning management
system at regular points in the semester: four times in 2023~Semester~1 and
five times in the other cohorts. \todo{Jos: what does the journal template ask
students to write about, and are journals graded? Describe briefly here.} Some
students write a paragraph; others write several pages.

Across the five cohorts there are 3,483 journal submissions, 3,071 of them in
the four LLM-coded cohorts. These counts are after removing duplicate records
from the 2024~Semester~2 export, in which the same submission appeared under two
different journal numbers for almost every student.

The analyses in this dissertation compare a team's journals, taken together,
with its peer ratings for the semester. None of them depends on matching a
particular journal to a particular peer feedback session.

The journals are self-reports written for assessment, so they are not treated
as ground truth about who contributed what. They are treated as an independent
second view of the team: evidence a human reader could use to decide whether a
team needs attention.


\section{Peer Assessment Models}
\label{sec:models}

Four models are applied to each rating matrix. All four take the same input
and produce the same output: an Individual Weighting Factor (IWF) for each
student, where 10 means an equal share. They differ in how they treat raters who
use the scale differently, raters who are themselves rated poorly, and
self-scores.

Throughout, $N$ is the team size, $s_{ji}$ is the score rater~$j$ gave
student~$i$, and $P_i$ is the set of teammates who submitted a rating of
student~$i$.

\subsection{Simple Average (baseline)}
\label{sec:simple-avg}

The baseline is the calculation COMPSCI~399 already uses
(Equation~\ref{eq:iwf}): a student's IWF is the mean of the scores they
received from their peers, with their own self-score left out,
\begin{equation}
  \iwf_i^{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{1}{|P_i|} \sum_{j \in P_i} s_{ji}.
  \label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}
The result is not rescaled. Its neutral value is 10, but a team's average IWF
can drift above or below 10, because points a student gives themselves are
dropped rather than redistributed. This drift is what the self-sacrifice attack
in Section~\ref{sec:attacks} exploits. When the baseline is compared directly with
the other three models, a second version rescaled to a team mean of 10 is used;
the ordering of students within the team is the same in both versions.

\subsection{WebPA}
\label{sec:webpa-method}

WebPA is an open-source peer-assessment tool developed at Loughborough
University~\citep{loddington2009webpa}. Its scoring method corrects for raters
who use the scale differently: before anything is added up, each rater's scores
are divided by that rater's own total, so that every rater hands out fractions
that sum to one. A rater who gives everyone high marks and a rater who gives
everyone low marks end up contributing the same thing.

Formally, the fraction rater~$j$ gives student~$i$ is
\begin{equation}
  f_{ji} \;=\; \frac{s_{ji}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} s_{jk}},
  \label{eq:webpa-fraction}
\end{equation}
where the sum in the denominator runs over the whole team, rater~$j$ included.
Each student's WebPA score is the sum of the fractions they receive,
$W_i = \sum_j f_{ji}$, again including their own self-assessment. The IWF is
that score relative to the team average,
\begin{equation}
  \iwf_i^{\text{WebPA}} \;=\; 10 \cdot \frac{W_i}{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k} W_k}.
  \label{eq:webpa}
\end{equation}
This follows WebPA's published scoring algorithm~\citep{webpa_workedexample}.
When some students do not submit, WebPA multiplies every score by a
``fudge factor'' of $N$ divided by the number of submitters. Dividing by the
team average in Equation~\ref{eq:webpa} has exactly the same effect, because
each submitter's fractions sum to one. WebPA also lets an instructor fix part of
the mark regardless of the peer ratings (its ``PA weighting''); here the whole
weighting comes from the ratings, as it does in COMPSCI~399.

On the COMPSCI~399 instrument, the correction has nothing to correct. Every
rater distributes the same $10N$~points, so every rater's total in
Equation~\ref{eq:webpa-fraction} is the same number, and dividing by it changes
no one's relative standing. WebPA therefore reduces to an average of received
scores, rescaled to a team mean of 10. It differs from the baseline in only two
ways: it keeps self-scores, and it is always rescaled. WebPA is kept in the
comparison for that reason: it is the established normalisation method, and
showing that the normalisation step does nothing on a fixed-budget instrument is
itself a result (Section~\ref{sec:webpa-result}).

\subsection{PeerRank}
\label{sec:peerrank-method}

PeerRank adds a credibility loop: a rater's influence depends on how highly
rated they themselves are~\citep{walsh2014peerrank}. It is defined by a
fixed-point equation in the style of PageRank~\citep{page1999pagerank} and
solved by iteration. The implementation here adapts Walsh's method to
points-distribution data. Let
\begin{equation}
  a_{ji} \;=\; \frac{s_{ji}}{\sum_{k \ne j} s_{jk}}
\end{equation}
be the share of rater~$j$'s peer points that went to student~$i$, with the
self-score excluded. Each student has a score $r_i$, updated in each round as
\begin{equation}
  r_i^{(t+1)} \;=\; (1-\alpha)\, r_i^{(t)}
    \;+\; \alpha \,
    \frac{\sum_{j \ne i} r_j^{(t)}\, a_{ji}}{\sum_{j \ne i} r_j^{(t)}}.
  \label{eq:peerrank}
\end{equation}
The second term is a weighted average of the shares student~$i$ received, where
each rater's weight is their own current score. The first term keeps part of
the student's previous score, and $\alpha$ sets how quickly scores move. The
starting score $r_i^{(0)}$ is the plain average of the shares student~$i$
received. Iteration stops when the total change across the team falls below
$10^{-6}$, or after 1{,}000 rounds. The final scores are rescaled to a team
mean of 10. Following Walsh, $\alpha = 0.1$; Walsh notes that the value affects
how fast the iteration converges more than where it ends up.

The adaptation departs from Walsh's formulation in four ways, each needed to fit
the instrument:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Walsh assumes each grade lies between 0 and 1. Here each rater's scores
    are converted to shares of their peer budget ($a_{ji}$ above).
  \item Walsh allows self-grades. Here self-scores are excluded, consistent with
    the course's own calculation.
  \item Because self-scores are excluded, the weighted average in
    Equation~\ref{eq:peerrank} runs over student~$i$'s peers only, not the
    whole team.
  \item The starting scores are rescaled to sum to one. Walsh notes that the
    starting point does not affect the result.
\end{enumerate}
Walsh also defines a generalised version that rewards raters for grading
accurately. That version is not implemented here, and the improvements Walsh
reports for it, measured on synthetic data, should not be read as applying to
the version used in this study.

The intuition is simple: if a highly rated student says someone contributed a
lot, that testimony counts for more than the same claim from a poorly rated
student. The concern, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:peerrank}, is that this
logic assumes plenty of evidence. In a team of five, each student is rated by
four teammates, so one unreliable rater is a quarter of the evidence, and the
credibility loop has very little data with which to tell a reliable rater from
an unreliable one.

\subsection{PeerHITS}
\label{sec:peerhits-method}

PeerHITS separates the two qualities that PeerRank ties together: how much a
student contributed (their \emph{authority} score) and how well they rate
others (their \emph{hub} score). The separation follows the HITS
algorithm~\citep{kleinberg1999hits}, which alternates two updates:
\begin{align}
  a_i^{(t+1)} &= \sum_{j \ne i} h_j^{(t)} \, s_{ji},
  \label{eq:hits-auth} \\
  h_j^{(t+1)} &= \sum_{i \ne j} a_i^{(t)} \, s_{ji},
  \label{eq:hits-hub}
\end{align}
where $a_i$ is student~$i$'s authority score and $h_j$ is rater~$j$'s hub
score. Both start equal for every student, and after each round both vectors
are rescaled to unit length. A good rater is one whose scores line up with the
students the team rates highly; a highly rated student is one the good raters
rate highly. Iteration stops under the same rule as PeerRank, and the final
authority scores are rescaled to a team mean of 10 to give the IWF.

Two implementation choices differ from PeerRank and are deliberate. First,
PeerHITS works on the raw scores, as HITS is defined on the raw link matrix,
whereas PeerRank works on budget shares. Some of the difference between the two
models' outputs therefore comes from this choice and not from the algorithms
alone. Second, both PeerHITS scores are updated from the previous round's values
at the same time, whereas Kleinberg derives each from the other in turn. Both
forms are power iterations that converge to the same pair of principal
eigenvectors~\citep{kleinberg1999hits}.

The practical advantage is that a modest contributor who rates accurately keeps
their influence over the weighting. Under PeerRank that student's low score
would reduce the weight of their ratings, losing information.

HITS has been used outside web search to judge both the things being rated and
the people rating them: for example, to analyse information-retrieval evaluation
results~\citep{mizzaro2007hits} and to study readers' ratings of scholarly papers
as an alternative to peer review~\citep{soprano2019hits}. To the author's
knowledge, it has not previously been applied to contribution ratings within a
student team, where each member is both a rater and one of the people rated.

\section{State Cascade}
\label{sec:cascade}

The four models in Section~\ref{sec:models} always return a number, whatever
matrix they are given. But some matrices do not carry enough signal for any
model's answer to mean much. If every rater gave everyone the same score, or if
raters disagree about who contributed more than whom, the models still return
weights, and nothing in those weights shows the difference. The state cascade
sorts each matrix into one state according to whether its ratings contain a
readable signal and, if they do, what shape that signal takes.

\subsection{Scale-free representation}
\label{sec:rank-transform}

Before testing for signal, each rater's scores to their teammates, self-score
excluded, are converted to ranks. If a rater gave scores of 15, 8, 12 and 5 to
four teammates, those become ranks 4, 2, 3 and 1. Tied scores share the average
of their ranks. The ranks are then divided by $k{+}1$, where $k$ is the number
of teammates the rater scored, so every rater sits on a comparable 0--1 scale.
This removes the rater effects that make raw scores hard to compare between
raters, such as leniency, severity, and how widely a rater spreads their
scores~\citep{linacre1989mfrm}. What is left is only the ordering: which
teammate each rater placed above which.

A rater \emph{qualifies} if they scored at least two teammates and did not give
them all the same score. A rater who gave everyone the same number expresses no
ordering and is left out of every step below.

\subsection{The three gates}
\label{sec:gates}

The cascade applies three tests in a fixed order. Each matrix enters at Gate~A
and leaves at the first gate whose condition it meets, landing in exactly one
state. \todo{Figure: flowchart of the three gates and the resulting states.}

\paragraph{Gate~A --- Is there an ordering?}
If no rater qualifies, the matrix is \textbf{Silent (flat)}. If exactly one
rater qualifies, it is \textbf{Silent (lone dissenter)}: there is an ordering,
but nobody to compare it with. Agreement between two raters is only measured
over the teammates they both scored, and only if they share at least three; with
fewer, a rank correlation says almost nothing. If no pair of qualifying raters
shares three such teammates, the matrix is \textbf{Silent (incomparable)}. In
all three cases the data are too thin for any test to be applied.

\paragraph{Gate~B --- Do the orderings agree?}
For every pair of qualifying raters, Kendall's $\tau_b$ is computed over the
teammates both of them scored, and the pairs are averaged. Kendall's $\tau$
measures how often two orderings put a pair of people the same way
round~\citep{kendall1938tau}; the $\tau_b$ variant corrects for
ties~\citep{kendall1945ties}, which are common here. The mean $\tau_b$ is
compared against a permutation null (Section~\ref{sec:perm-null}). If the
observed agreement is not significantly greater than chance ($p > 0.05$), the
matrix is \textbf{Contested}.

\paragraph{Gate~C --- Does anyone stand apart?}
The raters agree on an ordering, but does anyone stand clearly apart from the
rest? For each student, the \emph{consensus rank} is their mean normalised rank
across the qualifying raters who scored them. Two gaps are measured: between
the lowest consensus rank and the second-lowest, and between the highest and
the second-highest. Each gap is tested against the same permutation null, at
$\alpha = 0.05$, with no correction for testing two gaps. Four outcomes are
possible:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One at bottom} --- only the bottom gap is significant: one
    student is rated clearly below the rest.
  \item \textbf{One at top} --- only the top gap is significant: one student is
    rated clearly above the rest.
  \item \textbf{Both ends} --- both gaps are significant.
  \item \textbf{No standout} --- neither gap is significant: the raters agree
    on the ordering, but nobody is clearly separated from the group.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the order matters}
\label{sec:cascade-order}

Gate~B runs before Gate~C on purpose. If the raters do not agree on the
ordering, an apparent standout rests on noisy data: one rater's low score does
not mean the team agrees that person contributed less. Filing such matrices as
Contested before looking for standouts stops the cascade from reporting a
signal the raters do not share. The cost is that a contested team which
\emph{also} has a clear low scorer is filed only as Contested; the cascade does
not report it as both.

\subsection{The permutation null}
\label{sec:perm-null}

Gates~B and~C use the same null model. Each rater's scores are shuffled at
random among the teammates that rater actually scored. This keeps everything
about the matrix fixed except which teammate received which score: the team
size, who submitted, who rated whom, and each rater's exact set of scores,
including their leniency, their spread and their ties. It destroys only the
agreement between raters. Every threshold is therefore calibrated to the matrix
being tested, not to an outside benchmark.

The shuffle is repeated 1{,}000 times. If $b$ of the $B = 1{,}000$ shuffled
matrices produce a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one, the
$p$-value is
\begin{equation}
  p \;=\; \frac{b + 1}{B + 1}.
  \label{eq:perm-p}
\end{equation}
Adding one to the numerator and the denominator counts the observed matrix as
one of the possible arrangements, which keeps the $p$-value from ever being
zero~\citep{north2002empirical, phipson2010permutation}. The random seed for
each test is derived from the matrix's identity, so every result can be
reproduced exactly.

\subsection{From matrices to teams}
\label{sec:cascade-triage}

Each team has three matrices, one for each deliverable, and so three states.
For the analyses that work at team level, these are combined into one of four
team states:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Standout} --- at least one of the three matrices is One at
    bottom, One at top, or Both ends;
  \item otherwise \textbf{Contested}, if at least one is Contested;
  \item otherwise \textbf{No standout}, if at least one is No standout;
  \item otherwise \textbf{Silent}.
\end{enumerate}
The rule takes the most informative signal any of the three deliverables
produced, so a team is Silent only if all three of its matrices are Silent.
