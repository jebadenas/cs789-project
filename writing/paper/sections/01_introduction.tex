\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Budget: ~0.75 page.

When students build software in teams, staff mark what the team delivers and need some
other way to see how the team worked. The usual answer is peer assessment: students rate
each other's contribution, and the ratings adjust individual
marks~\cite{kaufman2000accounting,ohland2012catme}. Peer ratings answer one question, how
much each member contributed, and only at the points in the semester when they are
collected. They also depend on students rating each other candidly. Instructors report
students informally agreeing to rate one another
well~\cite{hooshangi2025assessment}, and a student who was shut out of the work by
teammates receives the same low ratings as one who chose not to take
part~\cite{hall2013freeriding}.

Many capstones also ask students to keep reflective journals. In our course every student
writes one roughly every two weeks, and the template has a section on the relationships
within their team. Students use it to describe arguments, uneven workloads and breakdowns
in communication. The difficulty is volume. One round of journals in the semester we report
on came to 222 submissions and about 370,000 words, or roughly 60,000 words for each of
the six tutors. Tutors read their own teams' journals to mark them, but a course
coordinator responsible for all 43 teams has no practical way to read them all.

We built a tool that reads one round of a team's journals and flags three kinds of
teamwork problem: interpersonal conflict, unequal contribution, and poor coordination.
It runs an open-weight large language model (LLM) on university hardware, so the journals never
leave the institution, and it raises a flag only when it can back the flag with the
students' own words, quoted verbatim. We developed the tool on four earlier offerings of
the course and then ran it on one round of journals from a live semester. Each of the six
tutors reviewed its flags for their own teams. No action was taken on the tool's output
during that semester.

Section~\ref{sec:context} describes the course. Section~\ref{sec:tool} describes the tool
and how its design changed during development, and Section~\ref{sec:practice} reports its
development and its use on the live round. Section~\ref{sec:reflection} sets out what we
would tell another course considering the same approach.
